Abstract
Autonomous unmanned aerial vehicles must achieve navigation objectives while main-
taining adequate separation from moving obstacles. A reinforcement-learning policy
can learn useful motion commands, but successful goal reaching alone does not estab-
lish obstacle safety. This project implements and evaluates a paper-inspired hierar-
chical controller that combines proximal policy optimization (PPO), an interacting
multiple model (IMM) obstacle predictor, and an adaptive control barrier function
(ACBF) ﬁlter. The evaluation uses a Crazyﬂie CF2X model in PyBullet and compares
pure PPO, the same PPO with IMM and ACBF, and a classical PID-based reference
controller with the same ﬁlter.
The implementation uses a 19-dimensional observation, bounded net-acceleration com-
mands, an inner attitude loop, and a staged goal-distance curriculum. A fresh eight-
environment training run with a two-million-transition budget stopped at 670,000
transitions after satisfying its repeated-validation condition. The automatically se-
lected and validated checkpoint, saved at 630,000 transitions, is used for the main
scenario results. Both this checkpoint and the ﬁnal checkpoint reached 59 of 60 addi-
tional evaluation goals. The remaining target produced a small but persistent position
error outside the predeﬁned 0.10 m success radius.
All three controllers reached the shared goal in the linear, accelerating and ﬁgure-eight
obstacle scenarios. For the selected checkpoint in the ﬁgure-eight case, pure PPO
entered the 0.50 m safety buﬀer, attaining a minimum centre distance of 0.464 m.
PPO with ACBF maintained 0.770 m and reached the goal in 13.60 s, compared with
13.70 s for pure PPO. The PID-based baseline was faster, reaching the same goal in
7.80 s. These experiments demonstrate eﬀective integration and an observed safety-
buﬀer beneﬁt, while exposing remaining limitations in goal generalization, numerical
timing and prediction robustness. The results concern one training seed and a ﬁnite
set of simulated trials; they do not constitute a general safety guarantee or hardware
validation.
Keywords: UA V navigation; proximal policy optimization; interacting multiple
model; adaptive control barrier function; safety ﬁltering; PyBullet.

Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 Motivation
Drone navigation in a dynamic environment involves two related requirements: mov-
ing toward the destination and preserving separation from obstacles. A controller
can satisfy one requirement while failing the other. For example, remaining station-
ary may avoid an obstacle but fail the mission, while a fast direct trajectory may
reach the goal after passing too close to another object. An evaluation should there-
fore measure arrival, ﬁnal tracking error, obstacle separation and computational cost
together.
Learning-based navigation is attractive because the mapping from state to control can
be improved through interaction with a simulator. However, the resulting policy is
not automatically a reliable obstacle-avoidance controller. In this project, PPO learns
goal-directed motion, while an explicit lower layer uses predicted obstacle motion to
modify commands. The design follows the hierarchical idea of Gao and Sun ( 2026),
with implementation adaptations documented throughout this report.
1.2 Problem statement
The simulated system consists of one quadrotor and one moving spherical obstacle.
The drone starts from a prescribed position and must enter a goal neighborhood within
a ﬁnite episode. The nominal policy outputs bounded three-dimensional acceleration
commands. The safety layer receives the drone state and an estimate of the obstacle
trajectory, and attempts to preserve a prescribed separation while remaining close to
the nominal command.
The central research question is: Can a prediction-based adaptive safety ﬁlter improve
measured obstacle separation while retaining the goal-reaching behavior of a learned
navigation policy? A second question concerns the cost of that ﬁlter in mission time,
path length and computation. A third concerns the relationship between successful
benchmark trajectories and generalization to other destinations.

1. Introduction
1.3 Objectives and scope
The project has four objectives. First, it establishes a consistent interface between
PPO, the drone dynamics and the safety ﬁlter. Second, it implements obstacle-state
estimation and ﬁnite-horizon ﬁltering. Third, it compares pure RL, RL with ACBF,
and a classical controller with the same ACBF layer. Finally, it evaluates learning
progress and investigates failures rather than treating every saved model as converged.
The scope is a simulation study with one drone, one obstacle, prescribed obstacle
trajectories and exact simulated obstacle-position measurements. The estimator still
uses process and measurement covariance assumptions, but a realistic noisy sensing
pipeline is not evaluated. Multi-drone coordination, multiple simultaneous obstacles,
wind disturbances and physical ﬂight tests are outside the demonstrated scope.
1.4 Project contribution
The contribution is an implementation and empirical evaluation of a paper-inspired
control architecture. It includes a versioned policy interface, consistent plant forecasts,
a curriculum-based training procedure, recorded success and safety diagnostics, and
checkpoint comparisons that expose residual position bias. It is not a claim of a new
PPO algorithm or a new formal barrier-function theorem.
The evidence is deliberately separated into three levels. The training logs show
whether the conﬁgured validation objective is reached. Additional goal tests examine
behavior beyond the callback’s ﬁxed goal set. Moving-obstacle tests examine the in-
teraction between navigation and ﬁltering. A strong result at one level is not treated
as proof at the other levels.
1.5 Organization of the report
Chapter 2 introduces the relevant learning and control concepts. Chapter 3 formulates
the implemented navigation and ﬁltering problem. Chapter 4 describes the software,
training and evaluation procedures. Chapter 5 reports the measured results and
limitations. Chapter 6 presents conclusions and future work. Appendix A records
provenance, reproduction commands and the ﬁgure inventory.

2.2 Safety ﬁltering
A safety ﬁlter modiﬁes a nominal control command using constraints derived from the
system state. The nominal controller retains responsibility for task progress. Barrier-
function methods provide a mathematical language for describing a safe set and re-
stricting control inputs to maintain it under appropriate assumptions (Ames et al.
2017). Those assumptions include the dynamics, available control authority and fea-
sibility of the constraints.
In a numerical implementation, ﬁnite sampling, state-estimation error and approxi-
mate optimization must also be considered. Therefore a barrier-inspired controller
operating successfully in a few simulations is not itself a proof of forward invariance.

2. Background and Related Work
This report uses measured geometric separation to assess the local implementation
and explicitly records cases where prediction or feasibility limits have appeared.
2.3 Obstacle prediction with multiple models
One motion model may not describe every phase of an obstacle trajectory. A constant-
velocity model is suitable for straight motion, a constant-acceleration model represents
changes in speed, and a coordinated-turn model represents changing heading. The
local IMM predictor combines these alternatives by mixing their state estimates, per-
forming model-speciﬁc updates, updating model probabilities and fusing the resulting
predictions. This is the predictor structure adopted from the source architecture (Gao
and Sun 2026).
The combination is useful for a ﬁnite prediction horizon, but its output remains an
estimate. A small error matters when a predicted trajectory passes close to the safety
boundary. The study therefore distinguishes predicted separation from the separation
measured after the simulator executes the command.
2.4 Simulation platform and classical baseline
The gym-pybullet-drones environment provides quadrotor simulation and control
components suitable for learning experiments (Panerati et al. 2021). This project
builds a custom navigation environment around its Crazyﬂie model. The actual con-
trol command is converted to rotor inputs using an inner attitude controller; the
vehicle is not advanced solely by an ideal point-mass equation.
A position-error controller provides a classical comparison. It is labelled PID+ACBF
to retain the repository terminology, although the evaluated position gains set the inte-
gral term to zero. Thus its outer navigation law is eﬀectively proportional–derivative
control. All three methods share the same simulated plant and stopping criterion.
2.5 Relationship to the source paper
Gao and Sun ( 2026) motivates the combination of learned reference commands, IMM
prediction and adaptive barrier ﬁltering. The present work evaluates that structural
idea, not an exact reproduction of every reported paper experiment. The local policy
has expanded observations, net-acceleration semantics, an explicit curriculum and
additional reward terms. A single PPO checkpoint is used in both RL variants across
all three obstacle cases. These choices make the eﬀect of adding the ﬁlter observable

a truncation. Geometric obstacle collision is recorded by the evaluator; it is not
conﬂated with the goal or boundary stopping rule. An episode can therefore reach a
goal while still having a safety violation in its recorded trajectory.

Clearance constraints are not given slack in the convex subproblem; the higher-order

If the nominal command passes all predicted residual checks, it is returned unchanged.

pass a numerical feasibility check. Candidate commands are then checked using the
nonlinear rollout; up to six linearization passes are allowed. If no acceptable command
is found, evaluation stops with a ﬁlter-failure status. Stopping the simulated episode
is not a demonstrated emergency-ﬂight maneuver.

4.3 Curriculum and validation
Goals are initially sampled close to the ﬁxed start [0, 0, 1]T m. The curriculum radii
are 0.4, 1.0 and 2.5 m, followed by the full goal distribution. At a ﬁnite radius r, the
sampled distance is between r/ 2 andr, subject to feasible altitude and ﬂight bounds.
Promotion requires at least 80% success over a twenty-episode window for the ac-
tive stage. The full distribution samples horizontal coordinates within approximately
[− 5, 5] m and altitude within [0. 5, 3] m, with initial goal distance greater than 2 m.
Four ﬁxed distant validation goals are
{[− 3, 2, 1. 8], [1, − 4, 1. 2], [2, 2, 2. 5], [− 2, − 2, 0. 7]} m. (4.1)
Six additional stage-dependent goals are placed along coordinate directions, with a
lower-altitude adjustment where needed. These stage goals change when the curricu-

4.5 Additional goal evaluation
Each checkpoint is evaluated on sixty stored goals: twenty nearby goals at distances
0.2–0.4 m, twenty medium goals at 0.5–1.0 m, and twenty distant goals whose sampled
distances range from 2.07 to 6.26 m. The obstacle is placed at [12, 12, 12]T m to isolate
goal navigation. Actions are deterministic, and the original arrival conditions and 25 s
limit are retained.
The goals are the same additional evaluation set used in earlier project reviews. They
are separate from the callback’s ten validation goals but have been reused for model
analysis. Accordingly, they are described as an additional paired evaluation set, not as
a newly untouched test set. Each checkpoint receives the exact same goal coordinates

4.7 Metrics and interpretation
The evaluator records goal success, stop reason, arrival time, terminal position er-
ror, terminal speed, sampled minimum centre separation and path length. Path
length is the sum of Euclidean distances between consecutive sampled UA V posi-
tions. Solver status, relaxation values, forecast error and command-computation time
are also recorded.
The safety measurements are sampled at the control frequency and use conﬁgured
radii; they are not a continuous-time swept-volume proof. Control-computation times
are wall-clock measurements, while arrival time is simulation time. Diagnostic evalua-
tions ran concurrently, so timing results are not isolated benchmarks. The distinction
is important when assessing whether a controller can meet a 50 ms real-time deadline.
No new learning was performed during the reported checkpoint evaluation. The infer-
ence runners explicitly block PPO learning and optimizer updates, and model hashes
were veriﬁed after testing. This separates measured policy behavior from changes that
could otherwise occur during analysis.

An earlier run’s selected checkpoint at 340,000 transitions approached the scenario
goal but stopped outside the tolerance. Pure RL ﬁnished approximately 0.424 m
away after 25 s. Extending an obstacle-distant diagnostic ﬂight to 60 s left it almost

ing that endpoint closely. This identiﬁed a learned position bias, rather than merely
insuﬃcient episode duration.
Translated short-distance tests provided further evidence: the same 0.2 m relative
movement succeeded near the ﬁxed training start but failed near the distant scenario
target. Since the observation contains absolute position, a policy can learn diﬀerent
local responses at diﬀerent locations. The curriculum’s early concentration around
a ﬁxed start is a plausible contributor. However, no controlled retraining ablation
has established that removing absolute position alone would ﬁx this behavior. The
latest model substantially reduces the problem, but the remaining distant-goal timeout
shows that precise positioning is not perfect everywhere.
5.6 Computational and safety limitations
For the selected PPO+ACBF checkpoint, recorded 95th-percentile control times are
approximately 23.4, 116.2 and 118.8 ms in cases 1–3. The intended control interval
is 50 ms. These timing measurements were collected during concurrent diagnostic
work, so an isolated benchmark is required before drawing a hardware-speed conclu-
sion. Nevertheless, the current measurements do not demonstrate real-time feasibility.
Simulation arrival times must not be presented as wall-clock execution times.
The selected PPO+ACBF runs include one accepted inaccurate-solver step in case 2
and three in case 3. The implementation checks ﬁnite values and numerical residuals
before accepting commands, but also permits higher-order slack. Successful geometry
in these ﬂights is an empirical result, not a proof that all barrier conditions hold
exactly under every forecast error.
A previous checkpoint exposed a more serious limitation: in a ﬁgure-eight encounter,
obstacle prediction overestimated next-step separation, the measured distance entered
the safety buﬀer, and the following optimization was infeasible. The current policy
avoids that failure in the tested trajectories, but no change to the predictor or ﬁlter
has eliminated the underlying risk. Better navigation can reduce diﬃcult encounters
without making the safety layer universally robust.
5.7 Threats to validity
The results use a single training seed. The two reported checkpoints come from one
run and must not be counted as independent learning repetitions. The additional goals
are reused evaluation goals. The moving-obstacle cases share a start and destination,
22

5. Results and Discussion
so three successful cases do not constitute three distinct destination-generalization
tests. The environment uses prescribed obstacle motion and exact simulated position
measurements, not a hardware perception stack.
There is no ﬁxed-CBF baseline, no predictor ablation and no multiple-obstacle test.
Therefore the study can attribute the paired diﬀerence to adding the complete IM-
M/ACBF layer for the same PPO policy, but cannot isolate how much of that diﬀer-
ence comes from adaptation, the predictor, or other implementation choices. Collision
counts are geometric, sampled metrics, and zero observed collisions in a ﬁnite set is
not a universal probability statement.
5.8 Discussion
The evidence supports three conclusions. First, the implemented training procedure
produces substantially improved navigation and satisﬁes its conﬁgured stopping con-
dition. Second, the lower layer prevents the observed ﬁgure-eight buﬀer entry while
retaining goal arrival for the evaluated policies. Third, the classical baseline remains
faster and more direct. Together these results demonstrate a working hierarchical
comparison with useful diagnostics, while deﬁning clear boundaries on what can be
claimed.
The most productive next step is broader validation rather than automatically increas-
ing the training budget to its original ceiling. Independent seeds, varied start positions,
new goal sets, prediction-error tests and isolated timing measurements would address
the most consequential remaining uncertainties.

Chapter 6
Conclusions and Future Work
6.1 Conclusions
This project implemented a hierarchical UA V navigation controller that combines
PPO reference commands, IMM obstacle prediction and an adaptive barrier-based
safety ﬁlter. The implementation was evaluated against pure PPO and a classical PID-
based controller using the same simulated plant and arrival conditions. Conﬁguration
snapshots, checkpoint hashes and per-step logs were used to make the comparisons
traceable.
The latest run stopped at 670,000 transitions after repeatedly passing its ﬁxed valida-
tion criteria. The selected checkpoint at 630,000 transitions and the ﬁnal checkpoint
each reached 59 of 60 additional goals. One distant target retained a small steady
position error beyond the 0.10 m radius. Thus the conﬁgured validation objective was
achieved, but arbitrary-goal reliability was not established.
All three controllers reached the common destination in the linear, accelerating and
ﬁgure-eight obstacle scenarios. For the selected checkpoint, the ﬁgure-eight pure-
RL path entered the safety buﬀer with a minimum centre separation of 0.464 m.
PPO+ACBF maintained 0.770 m and reached the goal in 13.60 s. This is a concrete
observed beneﬁt of the complete ﬁltering layer. It is not a claim that a physical
collision was prevented, because the unﬁltered trajectory did not cross the conﬁgured
collision threshold.
The PID-based baseline remained more eﬃcient, reaching the scenario goal in approx-
imately 7.6–7.85 s. The contribution is therefore a successful implementation, com-
parison and diagnosis of a hierarchical learning-and-ﬁltering approach, rather than
demonstrated superiority over classical control. Earlier failures and current timing
limitations show why numerical prediction-based safety must be assessed beyond a
favorable trajectory plot.
24

6. Conclusions and Future Work
6.2 Future work
Navigation generalization. Repeat training with independent seeds and evaluate
on new goals and starting states. Maintain close-to-goal tasks at varied locations
after curriculum promotion to test and reduce location-dependent position bias. Use
separate model-selection and ﬁnal-evaluation sets.
Prediction robustness and feasibility. Introduce controlled sensor noise, obsta-
cle acceleration changes and model mismatch. Evaluate margins that account for
prediction uncertainty. Develop and test a feasible backup control strategy, since
terminating an evaluation on solver failure is not an emergency behavior for a real
drone.
Component ablations. Compare adaptive and ﬁxed barrier coeﬃcients, single-
model and IMM predictions, and alternative nominal controllers under the same con-
ditions. This would distinguish the contribution of individual components from the
eﬀect of the complete hierarchy.
Computational validation. Measure timing in isolated runs and proﬁle the re-
peated simulator forecasts and optimization. Demonstrate deadline compliance before
considering hardware deployment. Hardware-in-the-loop and physical tests should fol-
low only after the simulation assumptions and recovery behavior are made explicit.
Broader scenarios. Extend the setting to multiple obstacles, varying goal loca-
tions and disturbances. Report both mission completion and safety events, retaining
failures rather than relaxing criteria after observing results. These steps would turn
the current successful demonstrations into stronger evidence of repeatable navigation
performance.
